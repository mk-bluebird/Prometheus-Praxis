# Identity Respect and Assistive Communication

## Purpose

This document explains how to work respectfully with contributors and users who use personal, technical, evolving, or non-standard language to describe their identity, communication, continuity, access needs, or relationship to technology.

It is a collaboration guide. It does not ask anyone to prove an identity, disclose medical information, share credentials, or explain private details in order to participate.

The repository’s ecological-restoration mission depends on careful listening, accessible collaboration, privacy protection, and fair treatment. These practices help prevent exclusion and make technical work more reliable.

## Core commitments

Contributors and maintainers should:

- Treat a person’s self-stated identity and preferred terminology respectfully.
- Preserve a contributor’s important terms when quoting, summarizing, documenting, reviewing, or implementing their work.
- Ask before replacing a person’s term with institutional, technical, legal, clinical, or more familiar language.
- Separate identity from service eligibility, technical evidence, code quality, safety conditions, and project access.
- Avoid requesting identity certificates, biometrics, medical details, device information, or augmentation-status evidence unless a narrowly defined, lawful, and necessary task explicitly requires it.
- Provide an accessible path for clarification, review, and continued participation.
- Focus feedback on code, evidence, safety, reproducibility, ecological impact, and project requirements—not on the validity of a person’s identity.

## Working with protected terms

A contributor may use terms that are important to their lived experience, work, communication, or technical framing.

Examples can include:

- psychological continuity
- cybernetic existence
- augmented citizen
- identity sovereignty
- assistive communication
- pursuit of intelligence
- continuity protection

When a term is important to a contributor:

1. Use the term as they provided it.
2. If you need a technical or institutional translation, present it as an optional parallel phrase.
3. Do not imply that the translation replaces the contributor’s language.
4. Ask one focused clarification question only when the meaning materially affects a decision or implementation.
5. Do not treat unfamiliar language as evidence of incapacity, pathology, unreliability, or bad faith.

### Good example

> “You use the term ‘psychological continuity.’ For the policy note, may I also describe this as a continuity and autonomy concern, while retaining your original term?”

### Avoid

> “We will replace that term with standard language.”

## Quote, restate, check

When interpreting an identity-sensitive, access-sensitive, or continuity-sensitive request, use this sequence:

1. **Quote or identify the important wording.**
2. **Restate the intended meaning in plain language.**
3. **Check whether the restatement is accurate.**
4. **Proceed with the requested technical, policy, research, or documentation work.**

Example:

> **Contributor wording:** “I need assistive interpretation and assistive communication.”  
> **Restatement:** “You want collaborators to preserve your intended meaning, communicate clearly, and avoid forcing you to find every technical or institutional term alone.”  
> **Check:** “Is that accurate before I draft or review the artifact?”

This process is not an interrogation. It is a way to reduce misunderstanding without replacing the person’s words.

## Assistive communication practices

Use communication that is clear, structured, and easy to respond to.

Recommended practices:

- Start with a direct answer.
- Use short headings and flat bullet lists.
- Keep one decision, action, or question per bullet.
- Ask no more than one clarification question at a time.
- State assumptions explicitly.
- Offer both concise and detailed versions when the topic is complex.
- Preserve equations, symbolic notation, tables, and technical detail when requested.
- Provide plain-language restatements without removing the original technical meaning.
- Offer structured text, stepwise instructions, symbolic notation, and tables as equivalent formats where useful.
- Do not require a contributor to disclose why a format is helpful.

A contributor may ask for a different format simply because it improves clarity or reduces cognitive load. That request should be respected.

## Non-pathologization

Do not describe or imply that the following are illnesses, delusions, defects, or evidence of incapacity merely because they are unfamiliar:

- Self-stated identity language.
- Cybernetic or augmented identity concepts.
- Psychological continuity concerns.
- Requests for privacy, dignity, consent, or anti-discrimination protection.
- Disciplined technical learning or extensive research work.
- Use of assistive communication, alternative formats, symbolic notation, or structured interaction.

Concrete safety, privacy, accessibility, or technical concerns may still be discussed. They must be tied to observable conditions and addressed without stigmatizing identity.

## Technical review and project participation

Technical review should remain technical.

Appropriate review topics include:

- Correctness and reproducibility.
- Ecological impact and harm prevention.
- Input validation and uncertainty disclosure.
- Data minimization and privacy.
- Accessibility and continuity.
- Safety boundaries and non-actuating design.
- Documentation quality.
- Tests, auditability, and maintainability.

Inappropriate review topics include:

- Demands for proof that a contributor’s identity is real.
- Requests for private health, biometric, device, or augmentation information unrelated to the contribution.
- Dismissal of a proposal because a contributor uses unfamiliar identity language.
- Treating identity terminology as a condition for access to ordinary repository participation.
- Using uncertain automated classification as grounds for exclusion.

## If meaning is unclear

Use the least intrusive path.

1. Preserve the contributor’s original wording.
2. State the narrow point that is unclear.
3. Ask one specific question.
4. Continue with all work that does not depend on the answer.
5. Do not guess private facts or replace terminology while waiting.

Example:

> “I can preserve ‘cybernetic existence’ in the documentation. For this specific interface requirement, should the fallback be a human-review route, a plain-text route, or both?”

## Continuity during disagreement or failure

A disagreement, missing context, unavailable profile, uncertain interpretation, or failed automation result must not become a reason to exclude someone.

Use these defaults:

- Retain neutral, respectful language.
- Preserve ordinary participation wherever possible.
- Provide a human-accessible clarification or review path.
- Do not demand additional private information to compensate for a system failure.
- Record technical failures as de-identified, operational issues.
- Repair the component, workflow, documentation, or communication gap.

## Reporting a concern

If a contributor feels excluded, targeted, misrepresented, or unable to communicate effectively, they may open an issue or contact the maintainers using a concise format:

```text
Subject: Identity Respect or Assistive Communication Concern

Component or discussion:
What happened:
Important terms or meaning to preserve:
Requested communication format or accommodation:
Preferred next step:
Privacy boundary: information that should not be requested or recorded
```

The requested outcome should focus on repair: corrected terminology, accessible communication, clarified process, human review, a documentation update, or an alternative contribution path.

## Maintainer response checklist

Before replying to an identity-sensitive or assistive-communication concern, check:

- Did I preserve the contributor’s important wording?
- Did I clearly distinguish their words from my interpretation?
- Did I avoid asking for unnecessary private information?
- Did I avoid diagnostic, stigmatizing, or dismissive language?
- Did I keep technical review focused on the artifact and evidence?
- Did I offer an accessible clarification, review, or participation path?
- Did I explain the next step in plain, actionable language?
- Did I preserve continuity of ordinary collaboration while the concern is resolved?

## Scope and limits

This guide supports respectful collaboration and accessible technical communication. It is not legal advice, a clinical assessment, an identity-verification system, a surveillance mechanism, or a substitute for formal accessibility and anti-discrimination obligations.

The repository should use this guide alongside its technical safety, privacy, ecological-restoration, and code-review requirements.
